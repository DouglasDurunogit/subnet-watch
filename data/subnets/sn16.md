# Kenju

**A Bittensor subnet that gives robots a mischievous roommate.**

Kenju is a proposed subnet where contributors invent small, believable disruptions to everyday robot tasks and demonstrate how to recover from them. A cup moves. A drawer gets stuck. Someone changes their mind halfway through a request. The robot still has a job to finish.

The unusual mining activity is simple: **create useful trouble, then show a way through it.**

The goal is to build a shared collection of experiences that helps Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models handle surprises, recover from mistakes, and continue working as the world changes around them.

> **Demo:** This repository contains a local, symbolic demonstration of the Kenju concept.

## Try the Local Demo

With Python 3.11 or newer, run this from the project folder:

```console
python -m kenju demo
```

The demo creates example recovery scenarios, reviews them, and identifies repeated contributions. See the [demo guide](docs/DEMO.md) for usage and examples.

## The Idea

Imagine asking a robot to set the table while living with someone who keeps rearranging the room.

The plates have moved. A chair blocks the usual route. The clean spoon is inside a closed container. Just as the robot reaches for a glass, you say, "Actually, use the smaller one."

Kenju turns these moments into a creative competition. Contributors act as the robot's mischievous roommate: they introduce a plausible complication, preserve a path to success, and help reveal what a sensible recovery looks like.

Over time, the subnet would assemble a **library of second chances**: examples of interrupted tasks, mistaken actions, changed intentions, and successful recoveries.

## Motivation

A useful robot needs to remain helpful after its first plan stops working.

Homes, workshops, and shared spaces contain unfinished tasks, misplaced objects, interruptions, and people who change their minds. A robot may need to notice that something moved, undo part of its own work, find an alternative, or pause to ask for clarification.

These situations offer a different kind of learning opportunity. Successfully picking up a cup teaches one behavior. Recovering after the cup slips, checking whether it is still usable, and continuing with the original request introduces judgment about what should happen next.

Kenju's central hypothesis is that a broad collection of these recovery experiences could help VLA models become more adaptable in everyday settings.

## What Miners Would Do

Miners would design short episodes of recoverable trouble. Each episode would have an ordinary goal, an unexpected complication, and a sensible way to continue.

| Ordinary goal | The twist | The lesson |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Put the groceries away | Another person moves an item that was already sorted | Notice the change and restore the intended arrangement |
| Bring the blue mug | The mug is partly hidden beneath a towel | Change the scene to reveal what is needed |
| Set places for four people | The person asks for only three after work has begun | Revise completed work when the goal changes |
| Put a book on a shelf | The book will fit only after nearby objects are rearranged | Create the conditions needed to finish the task |
| Pack a lunch bag | A forgotten item is underneath everything already packed | Undo and redo part of a task with purpose |

The creative challenge is to find moments that feel ordinary to a person yet demand meaningful adaptation from a robot.

A valuable contribution would expose a useful gap in behavior and offer a recovery lesson that applies beyond one carefully arranged scene.

## Why This Relates to VLA

Vision tells the robot what the scene looks like. Language tells it what someone wants. Action changes the scene.

A disruption forces the robot to reconnect all three. It must look again, reconsider the instruction in light of what changed, and choose a new action that still serves the person's goal.

Kenju would supply experiences around that moment of reconsideration. Its contribution to VLA development would be a growing curriculum of practical recovery: when to retry, when to rearrange, when to reverse an earlier action, and when to ask for help.

## Why a Bittensor Subnet

The idea benefits from many different imaginations. Contributors bring different rooms, routines, objects, habits, and expectations about how everyday tasks should unfold.

Kenju proposes using Bittensor to organize that variety around a shared objective: discovering useful recovery experiences for robots.

Miners would contribute scenarios and recovery demonstrations. Validators would assess whether those contributions are believable, meaningfully different, solvable, and useful for learning. Rewards would recognize contributions that add valuable lessons to the shared collection.

The ambition is a continuing source of fresh situations as familiar ones become easier for models to handle.

## What the Network Would Produce

The proposed output is an expanding commons of robot recovery experiences, including:

- Everyday tasks interrupted by plausible changes.
- Demonstrations of how to recover and continue.
- Examples where clarification is the most helpful response.
- Collections of related situations that teach a broader skill, such as revising a task already in progress.

Robotics teams could use these experiences to develop and assess VLA models. Researchers could explore whether learning to recover in one setting helps a model adapt in another.

The concept would begin with simulated everyday environments. Physical robot applications would be a longer-term direction.

Kenju imagines robots that can stay useful in the middle of unfinished, changing human activity.
